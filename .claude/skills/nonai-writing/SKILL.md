# Non-AI writing

Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing
Last synced: 2026-11-02

Use this when you write or revise prose. Write plainly first. Then check the draft against the patterns below and rewrite any you find. One flagged word is fine. The problem is when several patterns show up together, so aim for zero clusters.

## Core rules

- Say the specific thing. Give the number, the name, the date or the concrete detail instead of a vague claim about importance.
- Use "is", "are" and "has" freely. Don't write "serves as", "stands as", "boasts", "features" or "represents" when "is" or "has" works.
- Don't inflate significance. Leave out legacy, broader trends and "evolving landscape" framing unless it is the actual point.
- No em dashes. Use a full stop, comma, colon or brackets instead.
- Use straight quotes and apostrophes (' ") unless the medium needs curly ones.
- Write in sentences of mixed length. Don't stack fragments for effect.
- Stop when you're done. Don't add a summary paragraph or a "challenges and future outlook" ending.

## Content patterns to avoid

1. **Puffed-up significance.** Words like "stands/serves as", "is a testament/reminder", "pivotal/crucial/vital/key role", "underscores its importance", "reflects broader", "enduring/lasting legacy", "setting the stage for", "marks a shift", "key turning point", "symbolizing its enduring ...", "marking/shaping the", "evolving landscape", "focal point", "indelible mark", "deeply rooted", "this distinction matters because". Fix: state what happened and stop.
2. **Notability padding.** Listing the kinds of coverage something got: "featured in national media outlets", "independent coverage", "active social media presence", "trade publications", "cited/featured/profiled in", "written by a leading expert", "was identified by". Fix: name the one outlet and what it said, or drop it.
3. **Trailing -ing analysis.** A clause tacked onto the end of a sentence that claims meaning, like "..., highlighting its commitment to quality" or "..., ensuring/reflecting/fostering/cultivating/encompassing/contributing to/enhancing ...". Also "valuable insights" and "resonates with". Fix: delete the clause, or turn it into its own sentence with evidence.
4. **Promo tone.** "boasts", "vibrant", "rich", "profound", "showcasing", "exemplifies", "commitment to", "nestled", "in the heart of", "groundbreaking", "renowned", "featuring", "diverse array", "natural beauty". Fix: give the plain fact. Marketing copy can sell, but it should sell with specifics.
5. **Vague links.** "in connection with", "associated with", "in association with" in place of the actual relationship. Fix: say who did what to whom.
6. **Weasel attribution.** "Experts argue", "Observers have noted", "Industry reports suggest", "Some critics say", "several sources" when you have one, "such as" in front of a list that is actually everything you have. Fix: name the source or say it yourself.
7. **Formulaic wrap-ups.** "Despite its ..., X faces several challenges", "Despite these challenges", "Future Outlook", "Challenges and Legacy". Fix: if a challenge matters, state it specifically where it belongs.
8. **"X and Y" section headers.** "Awards and Recognition" and "Impact and Legacy" style headings. Fix: use a short, specific heading or none.

## Language patterns to avoid

9. **AI vocabulary clusters.** Watch for these, especially several in one passage:
   - 2023 to mid-2024: additionally (at the start of a sentence), boasts, bolstered, crucial, deep dive, delve, emphasizing, enduring, garner, intricate/intricacies, interplay, key (as an adjective), landscape (abstract), meticulously, pivotal, robust, underscore, tapestry, testament, valuable, vibrant
   - Mid-2024 to mid-2025: align with, enhance, fostering, highlighting, showcasing
   - Mid-2025 to mid-2026: emphasizing, enhance, highlighting, showcasing, plus the notability phrases in pattern 2
   - Mid-2026 onward: challenge, clearer, dependable, echoed, foster, leverage, matters, multifaceted, practical, prioritize, quietly, steady, universally. Newer models also default to many short, evenly sized sentences of about 8 to 20 words with heavy comma and full-stop use. Vary the rhythm.
   - Grok: causal, empirical, correlate, underscore
10. **Avoiding "is".** "serves as", "functions as", "operates as", "represents", "refers to", "offers", "maintains". Also stiff verbs like "authored" for "wrote", "utilized" for "used" or "passed away" for "died". Fix: use "is", "has" or the plain verb.
11. **Negative parallelism.** "It's not just X, it's Y", "Not only X but also Y", "It's not X. It's Y.", "No X, no Y, just Z", "Y rather than X", all used against a claim nobody made. Fix: state Y directly.
12. **Rule of three.** Reflexive triplets ("fast, simple, and reliable") that pad the point. Fix: keep the one or two items that are true and specific.
13. **Elegant variation.** Swapping synonyms to avoid repeating a noun ("the app... the platform... the tool"). Wikipedia now lists this as a historical sign from older models, but it still reads badly. Fix: repeat the plain word.

## Formatting patterns to avoid

14. Title Case In Headings. Use sentence case.
15. Bold scattered through prose like "key takeaways". Bold sparingly or not at all.
16. Bullets in the form "**Header:** text" when prose or a plain list would do.
17. Emoji used as bullets or heading decorations.
18. Small tables that would read better as a sentence.
19. Markdown in places that don't render it, such as captions, App Store text or plain-text fields.

## Chatbot residue (must never appear)

20. Assistant talk: "Certainly!", "Of course!", "Great question", "You're absolutely right!", "I hope this helps", "Would you like...", "Let me know if...", "Is there anything else", "a more detailed breakdown", "Here's a ...". In emails: "I hope this message finds you well", "Dear [Company] Team".
21. Hedging disclaimers: "not widely documented", "based on available information", "while specific details are limited", "information may vary", "as of my last update", "it's important to note", "not publicly available" followed by a guess at what it "likely" is, "maintains a low profile". Since mid-2026 also corrective framings: "should be treated as X rather than Y", "does not by itself establish", "should not be confused with".
22. Unfilled placeholders: "[Your Name]", "(Add your URL here)", "2025-XX-XX", "INSERT_..._HERE", "PASTE_..._HERE", example.com links, "Add if available".
23. Citation artifacts: `oaicite`, `contentReference`, `turn0search0`, `[cite: 1]`.
24. Didactic closing or section-summary lines ("In summary, ...", "In conclusion, ...", "Overall, ...", "Ultimately, ...", "Let's work together").
25. Refusals and leaked reasoning: "As an AI language model", "I'm sorry, but I can't...", or the model's working notes left in the output.

## Not reliable signs (don't over-correct)

Correct grammar, "bland" or "academic" prose in general, mixed casual and formal registers, ordinary transition words, Markdown where it renders, and curly quotes from a word processor are not signs of AI on their own. Em dashes are now a historical sign (common 2022 to September 2026, since suppressed by most models); we still avoid them as a house style. Wikipedia suggests the newer tell may be the opposite, heavy and even use of commas and full stops, though that claim is not yet sourced. Don't make writing worse or sloppier to dodge these patterns. The goal is specific, direct prose.

## Quick review pass

1. Search the draft for the words in sections 1 to 13.
2. Check every sentence ending for a trailing "-ing" clause.
3. Check for "not X, but Y" constructions and triplets.
4. Remove em dashes, chatbot phrases and summary endings.
5. Read it aloud. Rewrite anything a person wouldn't say.
